Topic #733932: "Not addressed" is a label too — what we learned reading 4,407 knee reports with an LLM
  Author: stevenleehans
  Posted: 2026-08-09 05:54:36.206000
  Votes: 20  Comments: 5

TL;DR — Only 58 of 4,407 training studies have real labels, so everyone manufactures
targets from the radiology report. An LLM does this measurably better than a regex
(0.8780 vs 0.8136 against the 58 gold studies). But the more useful finding is about
the gaps: 25.4% of all label cells are "the report does not address this", and what
that silence means is different for every finding. Getting that wrong costs more than the
labeller's accuracy does.

The observation that labels, not the encoder, are the binding constraint came from
measuring their pipelines — not from improving on them.

So what is this post for? Not the labelling. It is about what the labeller does when it
refuses to answer, which is a quarter of the time, and which we have not seen discussed.
If you already use one of the label sets above, section 4 is the part that may change what
you do with it.



1. The setup, for anyone arriving late

train.csv has a Report column. test.csv does not. So text can only ever produce
targets, never a model input. And only 58 of 4,407 studies carry real annotator
labels for the twelve findings — 1.3%.

That means almost everyone in this competition is training against labels they invented,
and the quality of that invention is a ceiling nothing downstream can pass.

2. The part we did not expect

We asked the labeller for a probability per finding, with an explicit "the report does
not address this" option that maps to 0.5. That turned out to be the most informative
design decision we made, because it makes the gaps visible instead of silently coercing
them to negative.

25.4% of all cells came back at exactly 0.5. And they are wildly uneven:

Synovitis is the worst column by a distance — and 84% of it is missing. Yet 27 of the
58 gold studies have synovitis. It is common in the joint and seldom written down.
The labeller is being honest, and the honesty destroys the column.



3. One thing we tested, and it worked

Radiologists report effusion readily and synovitis rarely, and the two co-occur.
So we asked a single pre-registered question: does the Effusion field predict gold
Synovitis better than the Synovitis field does?

LLM Synovitis field  vs gold Synovitis : 0.6780
LLM Effusion  field  vs gold Synovitis : 0.7115   &lt;-- yes
gold co-occurrence: P(syn | eff) = 0.63  vs  P(syn | no eff) = 0.22


A field that is not about synovitis predicts synovitis better than the synovitis field
does. Filling only the undecided synovitis cells from effusion — never overriding an
explicit statement — moves that column 0.678 → 0.790 and the whole key
0.8780 → 0.8873.



4. The part worth your attention: generalising it made things worse

The obvious next move is to do that for all twelve findings. So we did, properly: for each
finding, learn a ridge model predicting it from the other eleven, fit on the 4,407-study
corpus (never on the 58 gold — those were used only to score, once), and use it to fill
that finding's undecided cells.

When a radiologist does not mention a Baker's cyst, there almost certainly is not one —
3% versus 44%. The silence is the label. Overwriting it with a correlation-based guess
throws away real information. But silence about synovitis still leaves a 34% chance it is
present, so there the silence is genuinely uninformative and imputing helps.

Across the nine readable findings, that "silence ratio" correlates +0.59 with how much
imputation helped. (Nine points — suggestive, not proven.)


  
"Not addressed" is not one thing. For some findings it means absent; for others it
means nobody looked. Treating them alike is worse than leaving the gaps in.




5. Honest limits



58 studies is a small ruler. On it, differences below roughly 0.02 macro are not
measurable. We have had three separate readings from this ruler overturned by the
leaderboard — treat small gaps as unknown, not as zero.

A better key is not automatically a better model. We swapped these labels in and got
no gain on the first attempt; it only paid off after unrelated pipeline bugs were fixed.

We also tried the version that cheats — selecting which findings to impute using the
gold labels. It scored 0.8845, still below the disciplined 0.8873. Nothing was left on the
table by not cheating, which is not always how that goes.

Gold prevalence is the annotator's sampling, not disease prevalence. Every gold study
has at least one positive finding; mean 4.14 per study.

These are model-generated labels, not ground truth — a better approximation of what
the report says, nothing more.




6. If you take one thing

Ask your labeller for "I don't know" as a first-class answer, then look at where it
says so. The distribution of its refusals told us more about this dataset than its accuracy
did — and one column, repaired on that basis, was worth more than any change we made to the
images.

Labels and weights are public; questions and corrections welcome.
